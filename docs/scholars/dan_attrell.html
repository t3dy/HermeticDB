Dan Attrell

Scholarly Authority

Dan Attrell (b. 1985) is a Canadian scholar and translator whose doctoral training at Wilfrid Laurier University focused on medieval occult philosophy, Arabic-Latin textual transmission, and the history of astral magic in the Western esoteric tradition. His most significant scholarly contribution is the first complete, annotated English translation of the Picatrix — the most comprehensive and philosophically ambitious manual of astrological magic to survive from the medieval period — produced in collaboration with David Porreca and published by Penn State University Press in 2019. The Attrell-Porreca Picatrix has rapidly become the standard reference for anglophone scholars studying medieval astral magic, Hermetic talismanic theory, and the complex pathways by which Arabic occult philosophy entered the Latin West, filling a gap in the scholarly literature that had been felt since David Pingree completed his critical edition of the Latin text for the Warburg Institute in 1986. Attrell's contribution to the project extends beyond translation into the interpretation and contextualization of the Picatrix's philosophical framework, which draws on a synthesis of Neoplatonic cosmology, Aristotelian natural philosophy, and the specifically Arabic tradition of ta'thir — the theory of celestial efficacy — in ways that require substantial scholarly apparatus to make accessible to readers without specialist training in medieval Arabic and Latin intellectual history. His work is situated at the productive intersection of Islamic studies, the history of Western esotericism, and the philological history of medieval Latin scholarship, and it has opened the Picatrix's extraordinary intellectual content to an anglophone scholarly audience that previously had to rely on partial translations, paraphrases, and secondary accounts.

Central Argument / Scholarly Contribution

The intellectual significance of Attrell's translation project lies in what the Picatrix itself represents in the history of Hermetic and magical thought, and in the specific scholarly apparatus that the Attrell-Porreca edition brings to bear on making that significance accessible and verifiable. The Picatrix — whose full Arabic title is Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm (The Goal of the Sage) and which circulated in a Latin translation made at the court of Alfonso X of Castile in the thirteenth century — is the most comprehensive extant medieval synthesis of astrological magic, drawing on more than two hundred Arabic sources to construct a philosophical theory of how the practitioner can draw down and concentrate the specific stellar and planetary influences required for the construction of magically efficacious talismans. The text is simultaneously a philosophical treatise — arguing, on the basis of Neoplatonic principles of cosmic sympathy and the downward emanation of celestial virtue, that talismanic magic is a legitimate natural operation rather than a demonic invocation — and a practical handbook, providing detailed instructions for the construction of talismans corresponding to each of the seven planets and the twenty-eight lunar mansions, the specific materials, forms, and ritual conditions required for each, and the philosophical theory of why these operations should be expected to work.

Attrell's scholarly contribution is to make this complex, multilayered text accessible through a translation that is both philologically rigorous and philosophically sensitive — one that preserves the technical vocabulary of the original while providing the apparatus necessary for readers to understand the intellectual traditions on which the Picatrix draws. The Attrell-Porreca translation builds directly on Pingree's 1986 critical edition of the Latin text, incorporating Pingree's reconstructions of the most corrupt passages and his identifications of the Arabic sources, while translating into modern scholarly English that is accessible to historians of religion, philosophers, and literary scholars as well as to specialists in medieval Latin and Arabic philology. The annotations — particularly those identifying the philosophical sources of specific passages in Neoplatonic, Hermetic, and Arabic occult philosophical texts — constitute an important contribution to the scholarly understanding of the Picatrix's intellectual architecture.

The broader significance of the translation project lies in its contribution to what Liana Saif and others have identified as a major gap in the scholarly literature on Western esotericism: the underrepresentation of Arabic-Islamic sources and the consequent distortion of the account of how Renaissance magical philosophy was formed. The Picatrix is the single most important text for demonstrating the depth and sophistication of the Arabic magical tradition that entered the Latin West in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, and its availability in accessible, annotated English translation makes possible a more complete and accurate account of the transmission of Hermetic and magical ideas from late antiquity through the medieval Islamic world to the Latin West and the Renaissance.

Methodological Approach

Attrell's methodological orientation is that of a philologically trained historian of ideas who insists on the primacy of primary source evidence and on the importance of situating texts within their specific intellectual and transmission contexts before drawing broader historical conclusions. His approach to the Picatrix reflects the influence of David Pingree's foundational work on the transmission of scientific and astrological texts across linguistic and cultural boundaries — a methodology that attends rigorously to the specific textual genealogy of individual passages rather than making broad claims about cultural diffusion that cannot be grounded in specific textual evidence. Attrell supplements this philological orientation with philosophical sensitivity to the specifically philosophical arguments of the Picatrix's theoretical framework: he reads the text not merely as a collection of magical recipes but as a philosophical argument about the nature of the cosmos, the principles of astral causation, and the conditions under which human practical intervention in those causal processes is possible.

His approach to translation is deliberately transparent about its interpretive choices — the scholarly apparatus flags points of uncertainty, notes where the Latin text diverges from the reconstructed Arabic original, and identifies passages where Pingree's reconstruction is speculative. This transparency is itself a methodological statement: the Attrell-Porreca Picatrix presents itself as a scholarly instrument rather than a definitive final text, one that enables further research rather than foreclosing it. The annotations draw on the full range of recent scholarship on medieval Arabic occult philosophy, including the work of Liana Saif on Arabic influences on early modern occult philosophy and Kevin van Bladel on the Arabic Hermes, situating the Picatrix within the broader context of Arabic Hermetism and astral magic rather than treating it in isolation.

Key Works

The central work in Attrell's scholarly output is the translation: Attrell, Daniel, and David Porreca (trans.). Picatrix: A Medieval Treatise on Astral Magic. University Park, PA: Penn State University Press, 2019. The volume translates the complete four-book Latin Picatrix based on Pingree's critical edition, with a substantial introduction that situates the text in its historical and intellectual context, identifying its major Arabic sources, outlining its philosophical framework, and discussing its reception and influence in the Latin West from the thirteenth century through the Renaissance. The annotations to the translation constitute an independent scholarly contribution, identifying the specific philosophical sources — in Neoplatonism, Hermetism, Ptolemaic astronomy, and Arabic occult philosophy — of the text's major theoretical claims.

The introduction to the Penn State edition is particularly significant for scholars of Hermeticism: it provides the most accessible English-language account of the Picatrix's Hermetic philosophical framework and its relationship to the Corpus Hermeticum, the Asclepius, and the broader tradition of late antique Hermetic cosmology that was transmitted through Arabic intermediaries. The discussion of the text's account of how the soul of the magus — purified, focused, and aligned with the relevant planetary influence — functions as the instrument through which talismanic magic operates draws on both the philosophical Hermetica and the Arabic Neoplatonic tradition in ways that illuminate the Picatrix's position in the broader history of Hermetic thought.

Attrell has also produced scholarly articles and conference contributions addressing specific dimensions of the Picatrix's philosophical framework and its relationship to other medieval magical and Hermetic texts, extending the analysis of the translation into the broader scholarly conversation about medieval occult philosophy.

Scholarly Lineage and Disputes

Attrell's most direct intellectual predecessor is David Pingree (1933–2005), the American historian of mathematical and astrological sciences whose extraordinary command of Sanskrit, Arabic, Greek, and Latin made him the foundational scholar of the transmission of astrological sciences across the ancient and medieval world. Pingree's 1986 Warburg Institute critical edition of the Latin Picatrix — based on a comparison of all surviving manuscripts and incorporating reconstruction of the Arabic source — is the philological foundation on which the Attrell-Porreca translation rests. Pingree's methodology of rigorous cross-linguistic textual genealogy is the model for Attrell's approach to the transmission evidence, even as Attrell's translation project extends Pingree's philological work into the domain of accessible scholarly translation that Pingree's critical edition, addressed to a specialist philological audience, did not attempt.

David Porreca, Attrell's co-translator and colleague at Wilfrid Laurier University, has been the most important direct collaborator on the Picatrix project. Porreca's own scholarship on medieval Latin Hermetica — including his contributions to the Hermes Latinus series and his broader work on the reception of Hermetic texts in the medieval classroom — provides essential context for the Picatrix's Latin reception, and the collaboration between Attrell's expertise in Arabic philosophical sources and Porreca's expertise in medieval Latin Hermeticism has produced a translation that is richer than either scholar could have achieved alone.

The Attrell-Porreca translation stands in productive relationship to Liana Saif's work on the Arabic influences on early modern occult philosophy, which provides the broader interpretive framework within which the Picatrix's Arabic philosophical dimensions can be understood. Saif's argument that the Arabic occult sciences were a philosophically serious and internally sophisticated tradition rather than a mere conduit for Greek materials is directly relevant to understanding the Picatrix's philosophical ambitions, and her work on the concept of ta'thir and the Harranian Sabian tradition illuminates the specifically Arabic intellectual context from which the Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm emerged. The Attrell-Porreca translation thus participates in the broader scholarly project — associated with Saif, van Bladel, and others — of recovering the Arabic dimension of the Hermetic and magical tradition that has been underrepresented in a scholarship focused primarily on Greek and Latin sources.
