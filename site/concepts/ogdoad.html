Ogdoad (The Eighth)

THEOLOGICAL Concept · ACTOR TERM
Relational connections for Ogdoad (The Eighth)Read full encyclopedia entry →

The Ogdoad — from the Greek ogdoas, the number eight, designating the eighth sphere of a hierarchically ordered cosmos — is an Actor Term employed in the philosophical Hermetica to denote the realm immediately above the seven planetary spheres, sometimes identified with the sphere of the fixed stars, sometimes conceived as a supracelestial region inhabited by divine powers who hymn the divine mind unceasingly. In the cosmological map of the Hermetic tradition, the Ogdoad represents a crucial threshold: it is the point at which the ascending soul, having shed the daemonic accretions of each planetary sphere, arrives at a condition of relative purity but has not yet achieved the full divine union designated by the Ennead. Garth Fowden, in The Egyptian Hermes (Cambridge, 1986), identified the Ogdoad-Ennead pair as one of the distinctively Hermetic contributions to late antique soteriological cosmology, while Christian H. Bull, in The Tradition of Hermes Trismegistus (Brill, 2018), has argued that the specific numerical designations reflect the Egyptian priestly cosmological tradition of Hermopolis (Khmunu, meaning "City of Eight"), where the primordial Ogdoad of eight primal deities represented the state of chaotic potentiality before creation. The Hermetic adaptation of this Egyptian theological numerology transforms the Ogdoad from the chaos of the pre-creational abyss into the first station of divine order beyond the planetary cosmos.

Historical Usage

Corpus Hermeticum I, the Poimandres, contains the earliest and most influential Hermetic description of the soul's passage through the cosmic spheres, though it does not explicitly number the eighth sphere as Ogdoad. The text describes the soul's descent through seven spheres, at each of which it acquires a quality associated with that sphere's daemon, and then describes the soul's ascent through the same seven spheres, surrendering each acquired quality in reverse order. Upon completing this sevenfold shedding, the text states that the soul "goes to the eighth nature" (epi ten ogdoën physin) and there joins the powers that inhabit that realm, singing hymns to the Father in a language of pure, wordless intellection. The soul is then said to ascend further to the Father, entering into the divine itself.

The most detailed elaboration of the Ogdoad as a specific experiential state appears in the Nag Hammadi tractate The Discourse on the Eighth and Ninth (NHC VI.6), a Coptic text discovered in 1945 with no known Greek parallel. In this tractate, a teacher — Hermes — guides a pupil through a structured initiatory experience. The pupil first attains the Ogdoad, described as a realm of luminous intelligences who sing hymns of praise, and experiences an ecstatic transformation: he sees with a vision beyond ordinary sight, hears with an inner hearing, and participates in the celestial hymning. Hermes then leads the pupil from the Ogdoad into the Ennead, which represents a further intensification of this divine participation. The tractate ends with instructions to inscribe the experience on emerald tablets — an explicit reference to the broader Hermetic tradition of revealed writing — and to seal the text for transmission to worthy future readers.

André-Jean Festugière's discussion of the Ogdoad in La Révélation d'Hermès Trismégiste (vol. III, Paris, 1953) situated it primarily within the framework of Middle Platonic and Pythagorean cosmology, where the number eight (the first cube, 2³) had special significance as a symbol of cosmic order. Festugière interpreted the Hermetic Ogdoad primarily as a philosophical concept — the realm of ideal forms above the material world — rather than as a liturgical or experiential reality. Later scholarship has substantially modified this reading.

In the broader context of late antique religious thought, the concept of an eighth sphere above the seven planetary spheres was not unique to Hermeticism. Mithraism apparently involved an initiatory ascent through seven grades corresponding to the seven planets, leading to a divine realm beyond. Neoplatonic philosophy located the fixed stars in an eighth sphere and the intelligible world beyond that. Gnostic systems frequently described a cosmic hierarchy in which an eighth sphere (the Ogdoad) was the domain of the divine mother or the realm of relative transcendence over the seven archons. The Hermetic Ogdoad thus participates in a shared late antique symbolic vocabulary while deploying it in distinctive ways.

Scholarly Significance

Anna van den Kerchove's analysis in La voie d'Hermès (Brill, 2012) is the most sustained scholarly treatment of the Ogdoad-Ennead pair as a ritual rather than merely philosophical phenomenon. Van den Kerchove argues that NHC VI.6 demonstrates the existence of a Hermetic initiatory community that used structured liturgical procedures — guided meditation, communal prayer, hymn-singing, physical postures — to facilitate the experiential attainment of the Ogdoad state. On this reading, the Ogdoad was not merely a cosmological concept but an experiential goal that some Hermetic practitioners believed they had actually achieved, at least temporarily, through the performance of specific ritual acts. This interpretation aligns NHC VI.6 with the broader category of ancient initiation literature (Mithras liturgy, Apuleius's Golden Ass) rather than with the purely discursive dialogues of the Corpus Hermeticum.

Christian H. Bull's contribution in The Tradition of Hermes Trismegistus (2018) emphasizes the Egyptian genealogy of the concept. Bull notes that the city of Hermopolis was named Khmunu (City of Eight) precisely because of its association with the primordial Ogdoad — Nun, Naunet, Heh, Hauhet, Kek, Kauket, Amun, and Amaunet — the eight chaos-deities who preceded creation and from whose interaction the world emerged. The patron deity of Hermopolis was Thoth, the Egyptian god whom the Hellenistic tradition identified with Hermes Trismegistus. Bull argues that the Hermetic deployment of the Ogdoad concept reflects the specific theological traditions of Hermopolis, where the priests of Thoth would have been the primary custodians of both the Egyptian Ogdoad mythology and the emerging Greek Hermetic literature.

Wouter Hanegraaff, engaging with both van den Kerchove and Bull in Hermetic Spirituality and the Historical Imagination (Cambridge, 2022), places the Ogdoad concept within the broader Hermetic aspiration to recover the soul's pre-cosmic divine status. For Hanegraaff, the Ogdoad represents not merely a cosmological station but a mode of being — a state of existence in which the soul has recovered sufficient divine clarity to perceive and participate in the celestial hymning, even if it has not yet achieved the final union of the Ennead.

Transmission and Variant Forms

The Greek ogdoas was thoroughly naturalized in Pythagorean and Neoplatonic numerology before the Hermetic texts deployed it in a specifically soteriological context. The number eight, as the first cube, carried associations of stability, perfection, and cosmic completeness that reinforced its use as the designation of the first realm of divine order above the planetary cosmos. The Egyptian pesedjet — the Ennead of nine Heliopolitan gods — is a different configuration from the Hermopoline Ogdoad of eight gods, and the Hermetic synthesis that uses the Ogdoad and Ennead as sequential stations of ascent represents a creative integration of both Egyptian cosmological traditions with Greek Platonic philosophy. NHC VI.6 was not known to the Latin West until the twentieth century, so the specific Ogdoad-Ennead pairing as an explicitly numbered cosmological sequence did not circulate in medieval or Renaissance Latin Hermeticism. What did circulate was the underlying ascent cosmology of the Poimandres, transmitted in Ficino's Latin translation (Pimander, 1463), and the general Neoplatonic framework of a soul ascending through celestial spheres to divine union.

Related Concepts

The Ogdoad is structurally paired with the Ennead (the Ninth), which represents the higher divine sphere that lies beyond it and constitutes the ultimate destination of the ascending soul. Both concepts presuppose the prior liberation of the soul from the seven planetary spirits who govern the embodied life and whose influence must be relinquished before the Ogdoad can be attained. The experiential transformation described in NHC VI.6 at the moment of Ogdoad attainment is closely related to what Corpus Hermeticum XIII describes as the process of regeneration, wherein the ten divine powers displace the ten material vices and the person becomes genuinely divine. The Egyptian theological background of the Ogdoad concept connects it to the tradition of Egyptian priestly cosmology that also underlies the god making described in the Asclepius.
