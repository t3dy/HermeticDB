CH II: To Hermes PRIMARY_SOURCE

Tractate II of the Corpus Hermeticum, entitled "To Hermes" or in some manuscript traditions "God and the Divine Things," addresses the nature of divinity and the intelligible realm through a brief philosophical dialogue. This short tractate, preserved in all major Greek manuscript families of the Corpus Hermeticum, presents core Hermetic metaphysical doctrine through the voice of an unnamed interlocutor addressing Hermes Trismegistus. The text's brevity — approximately 1,000 Greek words in the original — has not diminished its philosophical significance; scholars including Brian P. Copenhaver have emphasized that CH II encapsulates the essential Hermetic vision of divine transcendence and the intermediary role of nous (divine intellect) in creation. The tractate exemplifies the Corpus Hermeticum's characteristic mode of presentation: the formulation of philosophical doctrine through dialogue and direct address rather than systematic treatise, creating an intimate pedagogical atmosphere in which the reader is invited to participate in philosophical revelation.

Content and Doctrine

Tractate II opens with the interlocutor asking Hermes about the nature of the divine and the intelligible realm. Hermes' response emphasizes the absolute transcendence of the divine — God is entirely beyond material existence, beyond becoming, beyond even thought as humans understand it. The tractate emphasizes that the divine cannot be apprehended through discursive reason but only through direct contemplation and stillness of mind. A key doctrinal passage contrasts the eternal, unchanging nature of the divine with the perpetual flux and change characteristic of the material cosmos. The text emphasizes the role of nous — the divine intellect or mind — as the principle through which the transcendent divinity relates to creation without becoming entangled in material existence. This doctrine of divine transcendence mediated through nous represents a Neoplatonic metaphysical framework adapted to Hermetic theology. The tractate concludes with Hermes exhorting his interlocutor to pursue knowledge of the divine through spiritual discipline and purification rather than through intellectual speculation alone.

Transmission and Manuscript Tradition

Tractate II is preserved in all major manuscript families of the Corpus Hermeticum, including the crucial fourteenth-century Parisian manuscript (Bibliothèque Nationale, lat. 6824) from which Marsilio Ficino's 1463 Latin translation was derived. The tractate appears consistently across the Greek manuscript tradition, indicating its early inclusion in the Corpus and widespread circulation in late antiquity. No Coptic Nag Hammadi parallels are known. Medieval and Renaissance scholars accessed the text exclusively through the Latin translation of Ficino, which became the standard version throughout the Renaissance and early modern period until the nineteenth-century Greek critical editions. Renaissance philosophers including Pico della Mirandola and Giordano Bruno cited CH II as an authority on divine transcendence and the role of nous in creation; the tractate's theological implications were particularly significant for thinkers developing Hermetic theology compatible with Christian metaphysics.

Modern Scholarship

Brian P. Copenhaver's authoritative English translation and commentary (1992) presents CH II alongside the original Greek and Latin texts, situating the tractate within the broader philosophical context of late antique Platonism. Garth Fowden's The Egyptian Hermes (1986) analyzes the tractate's doctrinal affinities with Neoplatonic theology and its role in articulating the Hermetic vision of transcendence. Wouter Hanegraaff's recent work emphasizes the continuity between CH II's metaphysical doctrine and later medieval and Renaissance Hermetic engagement. Christian H. Bull's The Tradition of Hermes Trismegistus (2018) contextualizes the tractate within the broader Hermetic literary tradition, distinguishing its philosophical theological concerns from the more mystical or visionary dimensions of other Hermetic texts. The tractate has been analyzed in relation to contemporary Neoplatonic philosophy, particularly the metaphysical systems of Plotinus and Porphyry, revealing both affinities and distinctive Hermetic emphases.
