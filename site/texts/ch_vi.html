CH VI: God is the Good PRIMARY_SOURCE

Tractate VI of the Corpus Hermeticum, commonly subtitled "God is the Good" or "God and the Good," addresses the moral and theological significance of identifying the divine nature with absolute goodness and the implications of this identity for human understanding of ethics and divine providence. This brief but philosophically dense tractate presents the Hermetic position that God is not merely a transcendent power or intelligence but fundamentally characterized by goodness, and that all good flows from the divine source while all that humans perceive as evil or deficiency results from material limitation and human error rather than from divine causation. The text exemplifies the Hermetic integration of metaphysical and moral theology, arguing that cosmological doctrine and ethical instruction are inseparable when properly understood.

Content and Doctrine

Tractate VI opens by directly asserting the equivalence between divinity and goodness: "God is good; in fact, goodness itself." Hermes elaborates this claim by explaining that the divine transcends all categories of limitation and negation; the divine can neither do harm nor wish harm since such capacities would imply imperfection and limitation. From this foundational doctrine, the text develops a comprehensive ethical teaching. All genuine good — beauty, truth, justice, wisdom — flows from the divine source and participates in divine nature. All that humans wrongly perceive as evil — suffering, injustice, disease — results either from human ignorance and improper action or from the material limitation inherent in the sublunary realm. The text emphasizes that understanding the divine goodness produces in the human soul a transformation toward goodness; recognizing the good's divine source cultivates virtue and moral excellence. The tractate distinguishes sharply between the divine will (eternally directed toward good) and material resistance and human misuse of freedom (the sources of apparent evil). This theodicy — the reconciliation of divine goodness with the existence of suffering — represents a central Hermetic ethical response to the problem of evil.

Transmission and Manuscript Tradition

CH VI appears in all major Greek manuscript families of the Corpus Hermeticum and was included in Marsilio Ficino's 1463 Latin translation. The tractate's relatively brief and direct formulation made it particularly suitable for quotation and adaptation in medieval and Renaissance ethical and theological writings. No Coptic version is known. The tractate's ethical and theological content made it a focus of attention for Renaissance Christian Hermetic interpreters seeking to establish the compatibility between Hermetic doctrine and Christian morality. The text was cited as authoritative evidence that Hermes Trismegistus himself taught concepts of divine goodness and moral virtue congenial to Christian ethics, a polemical strategy important for Renaissance defenders of Hermetic philosophy against theological criticism.

Modern Scholarship

Brian P. Copenhaver's translation and commentary address CH VI's ethical doctrines and their relationship to Christian theology and Neoplatonic philosophy. Garth Fowden contextualizes the tractate within broader late antique theodicy traditions and Neoplatonic ethics. Christian H. Bull analyzes the tractate's contribution to the broader Hermetic ethical vision. Wouter Hanegraaff's work situates CH VI within the framework of Hermetic spirituality as a comprehensive system addressing cosmology, metaphysics, and moral development simultaneously. Scholars have investigated the extent to which CH VI's ethical doctrine influenced Renaissance and early modern moral philosophy, particularly through the mediation of Ficino and Pico. The tractate's theodicy has been compared with contemporary Neoplatonic and Christian responses to the problem of evil, revealing distinctive Hermetic emphases on matter as the principle of limitation rather than absolute evil.
