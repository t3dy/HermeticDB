White Magic, Black Magic in the European Renaissance COMMENTARY

Paola Zambelli's White Magic, Black Magic in the European Renaissance: From Ficino and Della Porta to Trithemius, Agrippa, Bruno, published by Brill in 2007, represents the distillation of a scholarly career devoted to understanding the relationship between Renaissance natural philosophy and the magical tradition. Zambelli, who spent the better part of four decades investigating the Italian and German learned cultures of the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries from her base at the Istituto Nazionale di Studi sul Rinascimento in Florence, had produced a series of influential studies on Agrippa, Pomponazzi, Pico, and the boundaries of the permissible in Renaissance intellectual life before assembling them into this synthetic volume. The book's argument is, at one level, an extended response to Frances Yates: where Yates had argued that Renaissance magic was fundamentally a unified Hermetic-Neoplatonic tradition whose intellectual vitality derived from its recovery of ancient magical texts and whose ultimate significance lay in its contribution to the emergence of modern science, Zambelli demonstrates that there existed alongside and in tension with this tradition a powerful naturalistic counter-current — rooted in Aristotelian natural philosophy as it had developed in the Italian universities, particularly Padua — that sought to account for the same range of extraordinary phenomena through natural rather than supernatural causes. The two traditions — Hermetic-Neoplatonic and Aristotelian-naturalistic — were not simply opposed but were in constant philosophical dialogue, and understanding Renaissance magic requires attending to both sides of the conversation.

Content and Argument

The central figure in Zambelli's account is Pietro Pomponazzi (1462–1525), the Paduan and Bolognese philosopher whose treatise De incantationibus (written c.1520, published posthumously at Basel in 1556) she reads as the most rigorous and consequential philosophical engagement with the problem of magic in the Renaissance period. Pomponazzi's argument in that work is a systematic attempt to explain all apparently magical effects — including miracles, demonic possession, prophecy, and the operations attributed to the Hermetic magus — through natural, Aristotelian causes without invoking supernatural agents. His primary resource is the Aristotelian theory of occult qualities: properties of natural substances that produce effects disproportionate to their apparent physical attributes and that are therefore easily mistaken for supernatural operations. Zambelli traces how Pomponazzi's naturalism was at once a philosophical achievement and a cultural provocation: by explaining miracles through natural causes, he was implicitly challenging not only the magical claims of the Hermetic tradition but also the orthodox theological account of supernatural divine intervention. The response from both sides — from Neoplatonic magical theorists and from orthodox theologians — illuminates the boundaries of the thinkable in early sixteenth-century Italian intellectual culture. Zambelli reads the subsequent history of the magic debate — through Cardano, Della Porta, and into the seventeenth century — as a series of negotiations between the Pomponazzian naturalistic position and its alternatives. She also gives sustained attention to northern European figures, particularly Johann Trithemius and Cornelius Agrippa, whose engagements with natural and demonic magic represent a different cultural and theological context: the German humanist and the courtly occultist, both navigating the boundary between permissible natural philosophy and suspect supernatural practice in a social environment where the consequences of miscalculation — heresy charges, loss of patronage, exile — were severe. The book's treatment of Giordano Bruno reads his magical philosophy as the point at which the Hermetic-Neoplatonic tradition, pushed to its logical extreme, collapsed the distinction between natural and demonic magic that Walker had shown Renaissance thinkers laboring to maintain.

Methodology

Zambelli's method is archival and philological in the tradition of Italian intellectual history. She works extensively with manuscript sources, with unpublished university lecture notes, and with the correspondence networks that connected scholars across the Italian universities and between Italy and northern Europe. This archival grounding gives her conclusions a density that purely text-based analyses cannot match: she can show not merely what thinkers wrote in their published works but how they positioned themselves in ongoing scholarly conversations, how they responded to critics, and how their positions evolved over time. Her approach to the question of magic's boundaries is also social and institutional: she attends carefully to how the university setting — with its specific intellectual traditions, its relationship to ecclesiastical authority, and its culture of disputatio — shaped what could be argued and in what form. The Pomponazzi she recovers is not a lone rationalist anticipating the Enlightenment but a university philosopher working within and against specific institutional and intellectual constraints. This contextual sensitivity distinguishes Zambelli's work from Yates's more panoramic approach, and it is one of the reasons her conclusions have proven more durable.

Modern Scholarship

Zambelli's scholarship on Pomponazzi and Renaissance natural philosophy has been recognized as foundational by virtually every subsequent scholar working in the field. Brian Copenhaver's reassessment of the Yates Paradigm drew directly on Zambelli's work to argue that the supposed connection between Renaissance Hermeticism and the origins of modern science was undermined by the existence of a powerful naturalistic tradition — precisely the tradition Zambelli had documented — that could account for the phenomenon of Renaissance naturalism without requiring the Yates thesis. Hanegraaff's treatment of Renaissance magic in the Dictionary of Gnosis and Western Esotericism incorporates Zambelli's findings into its account of the relationship between Hermetic-Neoplatonic and Aristotelian approaches to magical operation. Walker's earlier framework — the distinction between spiritual magic operating through the spiritus mundi and demonic magic invoking personal spirits — is supplemented by Zambelli's addition of a third position: the Pomponazzian naturalism that sought to eliminate the supernatural entirely rather than merely to confine it within acceptable theological limits. Liana Saif's work on Arabic influences can be read in productive dialogue with Zambelli's: where Saif shows the Arabic astro-magical tradition providing the cosmological framework for spiritual magic, Zambelli shows the Aristotelian tradition — itself heavily indebted to Arabic commentators, particularly Averroes and Avicenna — providing the conceptual resources for the naturalistic counter-tradition. Together, the two bodies of scholarship substantially complicate the simple picture of Renaissance magic as a return to Greek Neoplatonism.
