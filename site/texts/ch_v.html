CH V: God is Manifest PRIMARY_SOURCE

Tractate V of the Corpus Hermeticum, customarily subtitled "God is Manifest" or "The Divine Manifestation," addresses the visibility and invisibility of the divine in creation and the paradoxical nature of divine transcendence coupled with omnipresent causative power. This brief tractate, preserved consistently in the Greek manuscript tradition, presents the cosmological doctrine that God, while remaining utterly transcendent and beyond all categories of being and non-being, nonetheless manifests throughout the cosmos through the operation of divine power and intelligence. The text exemplifies the Hermetic reconciliation of transcendence and immanence through the metaphysical concept of divine causation and emanation. CH V is traditionally paired with CH IV in manuscript transmission and thematic content; together these tractates present the Corpus Hermeticum's most systematic exposition of how the absolutely transcendent divinity relates to and manifests within the material cosmos without compromise to divine perfection.

Content and Doctrine

Tractate V opens with a characteristically paradoxical formulation: "The divine is invisible, yet clearly visible." Hermes elaborates this paradox by explaining that while the divine transcendent source cannot be perceived by sensory means, the divine power and operation are manifest throughout all creation. The text emphasizes that all things that exist — the cosmos, the celestial bodies, all living creatures, all movements and changes — are manifestations of divine activity and intelligence. Nothing in creation is void of divine presence or causative power. The tractate distinguishes between the invisible divine essence (which remains eternally transcendent and unknowable in itself) and the visible operations of divine power (which manifest throughout creation). This distinction preserves divine transcendence while affirming divine immanence and universal causative efficacy. The text emphasizes that recognition of the divine manifestation in all things constitutes a form of knowledge — gnosis — that transforms human consciousness and produces gratitude and reverence. The tractate's concluding exhortation directs the reader to contemplate divine manifestation in all aspects of creation and thereby to achieve spiritual elevation and insight.

Transmission and Manuscript Tradition

CH V appears in all major Greek manuscript families of the Corpus Hermeticum and was translated into Latin by Marsilio Ficino in his 1463 translation, from which subsequent Renaissance editions were derived. The tractate's relatively systematic exposition of cosmological and metaphysical doctrine made it particularly valued by medieval and Renaissance philosophers seeking authoritative statements on divine causation and natural order. No Coptic version is attested. Renaissance scholars including Thomas More and Pico della Mirandola engaged with CH V as part of their broader engagement with Hermetic theology. The tractate's emphasis on the divine manifestation throughout nature influenced Renaissance natural philosophy and contributed to the development of early modern doctrines of divine immanence in nature, though always mediated through Christian theological frameworks that emphasized God's transcendence.

Modern Scholarship

Brian P. Copenhaver's edition provides detailed translation and commentary on CH V's doctrinal content and its relationship to contemporary Neoplatonic metaphysics. Garth Fowden analyzes the tractate as exemplifying the Hermetic vision of cosmic harmony and divine intelligence pervading creation. Christian H. Bull situates CH V within the broader Hermetic literary tradition, noting its cosmological preoccupations. Wouter Hanegraaff's recent work contextualizes CH V within the historiographical framework of Hermetic spirituality as an operative system for understanding human relationship to cosmos. Scholars have investigated the extent to which CH V's doctrine of divine manifestation influenced Renaissance natural magic theory and the development of pantheistic and immanentist theology in early modernity. The tractate's relationship to Stoic and Neoplatonic precedents has been explored, revealing both affinities and distinctive Hermetic emphases.
