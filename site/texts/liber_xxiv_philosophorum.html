Liber XXIV philosophorum PRIMARY_SOURCE

The Liber XXIV Philosophorum (Book of the Twenty-Four Philosophers) is a brief Latin text of extraordinary philosophical influence, presenting twenty-four definitions of God in the voices of twenty-four unnamed philosophers, each definition a compressed metaphysical aphorism in the tradition of late antique philosophical sententiae. Composed in Latin — almost certainly in the twelfth century, though precise dating and authorship remain contested — the text was attributed in some manuscripts to Hermes Trismegistus and circulated throughout the medieval period in scholastic miscellanies alongside the Latin Asclepius, the Liber de Causis, and other philosophical texts associated with either Aristotle or Hermes. Despite its brevity — a few pages in modern editions — the Liber XXIV generated more sustained medieval and early modern philosophical comment than many texts a hundred times its length, principally because of its second definition, which Meister Eckhart, Nicolas of Cusa, Giordano Bruno, and Pascal all encountered and engaged with directly.

Content and Doctrine

The text frames itself as the record of a philosophical congress: twenty-four philosophers are assembled to define God, and each speaks in turn. The definitions vary in register from the theological-metaphysical to the mystical-paradoxical, but all share the compressed, aphoristic style of late antique philosophical summation. Definition I describes God as a monad generating a monad and reflecting ardor back upon itself — a statement of divine self-referential unity drawn from the Neoplatonic tradition of the One. Definition II, the most famous, reads: Deus est sphaera intelligibilis cuius centrum est ubique, circumferentia nusquam — "God is an intelligible sphere whose center is everywhere and circumference is nowhere." This sentence is the philosophical crux of the entire text and accounts for the majority of its reception history.

The second definition condenses a series of profound theological and cosmological claims into a single geometric image. The sphere is the most perfect of figures — the ancient and medieval commonplace — and an infinite sphere, one without circumference, cannot be localized: it has no edge, no boundary, no position in space. But if it has no circumference it also has no determined center — and yet the definition places the center "everywhere," making every point in the cosmos simultaneously the center of the divine. This is not merely rhetorical paradox: it is a rigorous statement of divine immanence (God is equally present at every point) and divine transcendence (God is not limited to or by any point), held together in a geometric image that the mind can grasp intuitively even when the logic resists full discursive formulation.

The other twenty-two definitions explore adjacent theological territory: God as the totality of being whose part is every whole; God as one who, in knowing himself, knows all things; God as the eternal present from which all temporal succession unfolds; God as the unity from which all multiplicity proceeds without God becoming multiple. Taken together, the definitions constitute a compressed survey of Neoplatonic philosophical theology in the tradition of Proclus and pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite — the tradition of apophatic and cataphatic theology that was the dominant mode of philosophical God-talk in the medieval Latin West.

Transmission

The earliest manuscripts of the Liber XXIV Philosophorum are twelfth-century, and the text was clearly composed in the context of the Chartres school's engagement with Platonic cosmology and the new translations of Arabic philosophical and scientific texts flooding into Europe through Spain and Sicily. The attribution to Hermes Trismegistus — found in some but not all manuscripts — was a reception-historical phenomenon rather than a claim of authorship: medieval readers recognized in the text's style and content what they understood as the "Hermetic" voice of philosophical wisdom beyond sectarian affiliation.

Alain de Lille (c. 1128–1203) is the first major transmitter: his De Maximis Theologiae (134 theological regulae with commentary) incorporates the second definition directly, giving it full scholastic legitimacy and ensuring it would be read by the academic culture of Paris and beyond. The printed edition of De Maximis Theologiae (Basel, 1492) brought the definition to a vastly wider readership at precisely the moment when Ficino's translation of the Corpus Hermeticum (1471) was establishing the Renaissance image of Hermes Trismegistus as the ancient sage of geometric wisdom. Meister Eckhart (c. 1260–1328) drew on the second definition in his Latin sermons and commentaries, particularly in the context of his doctrine of the divine spark (Fünklein) as the point where God touches the soul — a doctrine structurally isomorphic with the second definition's claim that the divine center is everywhere. Nicolas of Cusa's De Docta Ignorantia (1440) uses the second definition explicitly, attributing it to an ancient theologian, in the context of his argument that God, as infinite, must be the maximum and the minimum simultaneously — the center and circumference of all things. Giordano Bruno's deployment of the definition in his cosmological works goes furthest: Bruno universalizes the sphere to become the boundless infinite universe itself, with every point of the infinite cosmos equally its center, thereby transforming the theological aphorism into an argument for an infinite, centerless, heliocentric (and ultimately aheliocentric) universe.

The critical edition of the Liber XXIV Philosophorum was produced by Françoise Hudry for the Hermes Latinus series (Corpus Christianorum Continuatio Mediaevalis, Brepols), which is the standard scholarly text. Paolo Lucentini and David Porreca, as members of the Hermes Latinus editorial board, provide the broader scholarly context for the text within the Latin Hermetic tradition.

Modern Scholarship

Lucentini's work on the Hermes Latinus series established the philological foundation for modern study of the Liber XXIV and related medieval Latin Hermetic texts. His broader argument — shared by the Hermes Latinus project — is that the medieval Latin Hermetic tradition constitutes a distinct and coherent corpus that must be studied on its own terms rather than merely as a debased antechamber to the Renaissance rediscovery of Greek Hermetic texts. David Porreca's research on the medieval classroom reception of Hermes texts has documented how the Liber XXIV was used not as an esoteric document but as a philosophical teaching text with full scholastic apparatus — student glosses, marginal commentaries, and integration into standard philosophical curricula. Hanegraaff's treatment in multiple publications situates the Liber XXIV within the broader history of Western esotericism and specifically within the Hermetic tradition's claim to a wisdom that transcends and precedes revealed religion.
