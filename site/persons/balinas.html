Apollonius of Tyana (Balinas) MYTHICAL_FIGURE

Balinas (Arabic: Bālīnās; also Ballīnās, Abū Yūsuf Yaʿqūb ibn Isḥāq al-Kindī calls him Bālīnās al-Ḥakīm, "Balinas the Wise") is the Arabic name given to the pseudonymous author of the Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa wa-Ṣanʿat al-Ṭabīʿa — the Book of the Secret of Creation and the Art of Nature, also known as the Book of Causes (Kitāb al-ʿIlal) — one of the most influential texts in the Arabic-language Hermetic and alchemical tradition and the work that contains the oldest surviving version of the Emerald Tablet (Tabula Smaragdina), the most widely cited alchemical text in Western history. The name Balinas is a Arabization of Apollonius of Tyana, the first-century CE Neopythagorean holy man and wonder-worker from Cappadocia, and the text presents itself as a translation from Greek of a work discovered by Apollonius in a subterranean vault beneath a statue of Hermes Trismegistus — a narrative that places it squarely within the genre of the "discovered book" (heurema), the literary convention of ancient and late antique revelatory literature in which authoritative knowledge is presented as having been hidden and then miraculously recovered. Modern scholarship, following the foundational studies of Ursula Weisser and Kevin van Bladel, has established that the Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa is almost certainly an Arabic composition, probably of late antique or early Islamic date (most likely between the late sixth and early ninth centuries CE), drawing on Greek, Syriac, and perhaps Pahlavi sources, and that no Greek original by Apollonius of Tyana underlies it.

The Text and Its Sources

The Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa is a substantial encyclopedic work covering natural philosophy, medicine, mineralogy, botany, and the properties of animals and stones. Its framework is cosmological and theological: all of nature is understood through the lens of the four elements and their qualities, through the influence of the celestial spheres on terrestrial matter, and through the operation of a universal spirit (al-rūḥāniyya) that permeates and animates the natural world. This spirit is the key to the "secret of creation" announced in the title — the understanding of which enables the adept to work with nature rather than against it, to harness natural sympathies and antipathies, and ultimately to produce transformative effects in matter, including the transmutation of base metals.

The text's sources are multiple and not always easy to disentangle. Greek natural philosophical traditions — Stoic pneumatology, Aristotelian hylomorphism, and Neoplatonic emanationism — are all present. So is the tradition of Greek and Hellenistic scientific writing on stones, plants, and animals (the lapidary, botanical, and zoological traditions), much of which circulated in late antique compilations. Syriac Christian natural philosophy may have served as an intermediary. What is distinctive about the Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa is the way these diverse sources are synthesized within a revelatory and theurgical framework in which Hermes Trismegistus is the supreme authority — not merely as a philosophical teacher but as a divine sage who has left encoded knowledge in the natural world for those with eyes to read it.

The Emerald Tablet (Lawḥ al-zumurrud in Arabic) appears in the text as a caption or inscription found on a green tablet placed in the hands of a statue of Hermes in the underground vault where Apollonius/Balinas discovers the book. Its contents — in the Balinas version — present a compressed cosmological and alchemical doctrine: the unity of upper and lower principles, the role of the Sun and Moon as cosmic generative forces, the operation of the universal spirit in producing natural effects, and the identification of all natural processes with a single underlying principle of transformation. The phrase that became the most famous in Western alchemy — quod est inferius est sicut quod est superius, "what is below is like what is above" — is present in the Arabic original in closely parallel form. The chain of transmission from the Arabic Balinas version to the Latin West runs through multiple translation events: the Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa was translated into Latin by Hugo of Santalla around 1140, and the Emerald Tablet circulated separately in Latin versions from the twelfth century onward, becoming one of the most copied, commented on, and debated texts in the entire alchemical tradition.

Hermetic Significance

Balinas stands at the intersection of two of the most important dynamics in the transmission of Hermeticism: the Arabic reception and transformation of Greek and late antique Hermetic material, and the transmission of that transformed material into the Latin West through the translation movement of the twelfth century. In both respects, the Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa is a primary document, not a marginal one. The text's framing device — Apollonius discovering a secret book beneath a statue of Hermes Trismegistus — establishes a narrative of recovered ancient wisdom that will recur throughout the Arabic and later Latin Hermetic traditions, and that provides the archetypal template for many of the "discovery narratives" that structure Renaissance Hermetic claims to antiquity.

The doctrine of the universal spirit (al-rūḥāniyya) in the Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa is particularly significant. It provides a theoretical basis for the sympathetic connections between celestial and terrestrial realms that underlie both astrological and alchemical practice: if a single animating spirit pervades all of nature, then the influence of the stars on metals, plants, and human bodies is not a matter of mysterious action at a distance but of the continuous operation of a common medium. This doctrine was enormously influential on subsequent Arabic occult philosophy, including the tradition associated with the Ikhwan al-Safa (Brethren of Purity) and the major works of natural magic transmitted under the title Picatrix (Arabic Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm). It also provided the theoretical foundation for the Renaissance tradition of magia naturalis as developed by Marsilio Ficino, Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, and Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, for whom the operability of natural magic depended on exactly this kind of universal sympathy mediated by spirit.

The Emerald Tablet's specific influence on Western alchemy and Hermeticism is incalculable. It was commented on by Albert the Great, Roger Bacon, and virtually every major alchemical author of the medieval and early modern periods. Newton's partial translation and extended commentary (the so-called "Keynes MS" 28) reveals that even the founder of classical mechanics understood his natural philosophical work as continuous with the decipherment of Hermetic nature-codes embedded in ancient authoritative texts. The tablet's doctrine of the correspondence between above and below became a foundational axiom of the Western esoteric tradition in its broadest sense, surfacing in Renaissance Neoplatonism, in the Rosicrucian manifestos, in eighteenth-century Masonic symbolism, and in nineteenth-century occultism.

Scholarly Debates

The primary scholarly controversies surrounding Balinas concern the dating, provenance, and sources of the Kitāb Sirr al-Khalīqa. Ursula Weisser's comprehensive 1980 study, Buch über das Geheimnis der Schöpfung und die Darstellung der Natur, established the Arabic text on a critical basis and argued for a date of composition between the sixth and ninth centuries CE, probably in a Syriac Christian milieu drawing on Greek sources. Kevin van Bladel's The Arabic Hermes (2009) refined this picture by situating the text within the broader context of Arabic Hermetica and arguing for a more specifically early Islamic dating, while emphasizing the centrality of Syriac intermediary transmission. The question of whether a Greek original underlies any portion of the text — and in particular whether the Emerald Tablet itself has a Greek antecedent — has been answered in the negative by most recent scholarship, though the discovery of additional manuscript evidence could in principle alter this conclusion.
