Kocku von Stuckrad SCHOLAR

Kocku von Stuckrad (b. 1966) is a German scholar of religious studies and professor at the University of Groningen whose application of discourse-analytical methods to the history of Western esotericism has made him one of the field's most theoretically innovative voices. Educated in Germany and having held positions at the Free University of Berlin and the University of Amsterdam before settling at Groningen, von Stuckrad works at the intersection of religious studies, cultural history, and the theory of knowledge, with particular interests in the history of astrology, the construction of esoteric knowledge claims, and the relationship between science and religion in European modernity. His significance for Hermetic studies lies less in his engagement with specific Hermetic texts than in his theoretical reframing of what scholars mean when they speak of "Western esotericism" as a category of inquiry: rather than treating esotericism as a bounded tradition with determinate members, von Stuckrad proposes analyzing it as a discursive formation — a set of recurring rhetorical strategies for claiming a special, elevated form of knowledge — that can appear in many different historical and cultural contexts without necessarily constituting a coherent tradition. This Foucauldian reframing has opened productive debates about the limits of the typological and historical approaches that have dominated the field since Antoine Faivre's foundational work in the 1990s.

Central Argument

Von Stuckrad's central argument, developed most fully in Locations of Knowledge in Medieval and Early Modern Europe: Esoteric Discourse and Western Identities (Brill, 2010), is that "Western esotericism" is best understood not as a specific tradition of texts, practices, and beliefs with determinate historical boundaries, but as a discursive formation constituted by recurring claims to possess a superior, hidden, or direct form of knowledge. Drawing on Michel Foucault's concept of discourse — the system of rules that governs what can be said, by whom, and in what contexts — von Stuckrad argues that what historians have called "esoteric" traditions are united not by shared doctrines or practices but by shared rhetorical strategies: claims to a higher knowledge inaccessible to ordinary cognition, appeals to a hidden tradition of transmission, and the construction of an elite of knowers distinguished from an uninitiated majority. This framing has several significant implications. First, it dissolves the question of whether a given text or figure "really" belongs to the esoteric tradition, since membership in the tradition is a function of discursive positioning rather than doctrinal content. Second, it allows the analyst to trace esoteric discourse across contexts — scientific, religious, philosophical, literary — without being bound by the typological criteria Faivre developed. Third, it situates the study of esotericism within the broader history of knowledge production and the social dynamics that determine which forms of knowledge claim legitimacy in a given cultural moment. Von Stuckrad's earlier monograph Western Esotericism: A Brief History of Secret Knowledge (Equinox, 2005), translated from the German Das Ringen um die Astrologie (de Gruyter, 2000), presented a more accessible version of this argument directed at a broader audience.

Methodological Approach

Von Stuckrad's methodological approach is explicitly Foucauldian: he treats texts and traditions as elements of discourse rather than as transparent expressions of the beliefs of their authors, and he is concerned above all with the social and institutional conditions under which particular knowledge claims achieve or lose legitimacy. His engagement with Foucault is complemented by the sociology of knowledge (particularly Bourdieu's field theory), science studies, and the history of concepts in the tradition of Koselleck. This theoretical apparatus distinguishes his work from both the typological approach of Faivre and the historicist empiricism of Hanegraaff, who prioritizes reconstruction of what individual historical actors actually believed and argued over analysis of the discursive formations within which they operated. In practice, von Stuckrad's discourse-analytical approach leads him to focus on moments of boundary-drawing — episodes in which the boundary between legitimate and illegitimate knowledge is constructed, contested, or redefined — rather than on the internal content of specific Hermetic texts. His book on the history of astrology, A History of Astrology: From Mesopotamia to the Present (Equinox, 2005; German original 2003), exemplifies this approach: it is less concerned with the technical details of astrological systems than with the shifting social and epistemic status of astrological knowledge claims across different historical contexts.

Key Works

Western Esotericism: A Brief History of Secret Knowledge (London: Equinox, 2005) is von Stuckrad's most widely read work and serves as the clearest introduction to his discursive approach. The book covers the major figures and traditions of Western esoteric thought from Late Antiquity through the twentieth century, but consistently organizes its material around the question of how esoteric knowledge claims function socially and rhetorically rather than around the internal doctrinal development of specific traditions. Locations of Knowledge in Medieval and Early Modern Europe: Esoteric Discourse and Western Identities (Leiden: Brill, 2010) offers von Stuckrad's most fully developed theoretical account and applies the discourse-analytical framework to specific case studies from the medieval and early modern periods, including astrological, alchemical, and Kabbalistic traditions. The Scientification of Religion: An Historical Study of Discursive Change, 1800–2000 (Berlin: de Gruyter, 2014) extends the analytical framework into the modern period, examining how religious and esoteric knowledge claims have been reformulated in the language of science since the nineteenth century — a process relevant to the modern reception of Hermetic ideas in Theosophy, anthroposophy, and related movements.

Scholarly Lineage and Disputes

Von Stuckrad's most significant intellectual interlocutor is Wouter J. Hanegraaff, with whom he shares the project of establishing Western esotericism as a legitimate field of academic inquiry but from whom he diverges substantially in method. Hanegraaff's critique of von Stuckrad's approach centers on what he sees as its tendency to dissolve the historical specificity of individual actors and texts into broad discursive patterns: if esotericism is merely a recurring rhetoric of superior knowledge claims, it becomes difficult to distinguish between very different phenomena that share the rhetoric while differing entirely in content, context, and consequence. Von Stuckrad, conversely, regards Hanegraaff's historicism as insufficiently attentive to the social and institutional dynamics that govern the production and reception of knowledge claims. The debate between them reflects a broader methodological tension in the humanities between actor-centered historical reconstruction and structuralist or post-structuralist attention to discourse, power, and the conditions of possibility for knowledge. Von Stuckrad also engages critically with Antoine Faivre's typological approach, arguing that the four characteristics Faivre uses to define esotericism are too culturally specific to serve as a cross-cultural analytical framework. His own discursive approach, he argues, avoids this essentialist trap by treating esotericism as a rhetorical strategy rather than a doctrinal type.
