Florian Ebeling SCHOLAR

Florian Ebeling, German historian of religion and esotericism, is the author of Das Geheimnis des Hermes Trismegistos: Geschichte des Hermetismus (Munich: C.H. Beck, 2005), translated into English by David Lorton as The Secret History of Hermes Trismegistus: Hermeticism from Ancient to Modern Times (Ithaca: Cornell University Press, 2007). This volume represents the most concise and methodologically explicit synthetic account of the Hermetic tradition as a whole available in any language at the time of its publication. Working within the historiographical framework established by Wouter Hanegraaff, Ebeling organises the long history of Hermetism from its Egyptian antecedents through the Corpus Hermeticum's Late Antique composition, the Arabic and medieval Latin transmission, the Renaissance rediscovery, and the modern period of Theosophy and New Age reception. The book is distinctive for its consistent methodological self-consciousness: it does not treat Hermeticism as an essence persisting unchanged from ancient Egypt to the present day, but traces the shifting cultural and intellectual functions that the name and texts of Hermes Trismegistus have been made to serve across radically different historical contexts.

Central Argument

Ebeling's central argument is that the figure of Hermes Trismegistus is not primarily a theological or philosophical claim but a cultural function: across its many different historical contexts, the name "Hermes Trismegistus" has served to authorise claims to ancient wisdom that are in fact products of their own period. This begins with the Late Antique Egyptian priestly milieu in which the philosophical Hermetica were actually composed — texts presenting themselves as ancient Egyptian wisdom while in fact articulating a synthesis of Middle Platonism, Stoic cosmology, and Egyptian religious tradition. It continues through Isaac Casaubon's famous 1614 philological demonstration that the Corpus Hermeticum postdated early Christianity — which destroyed the Hermetic texts' claim to prisca theologia authority while doing nothing to diminish their actual influence on European thought. And it extends into the modern period, where Theosophical and New Age movements have continued to invoke Hermes as a figure of perennial wisdom independently of the philological and historical scholarship that has reconstructed the actual history of the texts. Ebeling insists throughout that understanding the gap between the claimed and actual history of the Hermetic tradition is not debunking but a prerequisite for taking the tradition seriously as a historical phenomenon.

Methodological Approach and Key Works

The Secret History of Hermes Trismegistus (2007) follows the chronological transmission of the Hermetic corpus from the Egyptian priestly context reconstructed by Garth Fowden's The Egyptian Hermes (1986), through the Renaissance rediscovery focused on Marsilio Ficino's 1463 Latin translation and its consequences for Florentine Neoplatonism, through the Casaubon refutation and its complex aftermath, to the 20th-century reception in Jungian psychology, Theosophy, and New Age spirituality. Ebeling's methodological approach is historicist throughout: he situates each phase of the tradition's reception in its specific intellectual and social context, refusing both the Perennialist view that Hermetism transmits a timeless wisdom and the dismissive Enlightenment view that it is simply error. The book's brevity — under two hundred pages — makes it an ideal entry point for students approaching the Hermetic tradition, while its footnotes direct specialists to primary literature and to the most important recent scholarship.

Scholarly Lineage and Disputes

Ebeling's work is best understood as a synthesis and popularisation of the Amsterdam school's historiographical framework rather than as an independent research programme. His primary debts are to Hanegraaff's methodological writings, to Fowden's The Egyptian Hermes, and to Brian Copenhaver's scholarship on the Renaissance Hermetica. The book does not advance new archival findings; its contribution is to make the current state of scholarly understanding accessible to a broad audience without sacrificing methodological rigour. In the German academic context, it represents the first systematic application of the Amsterdam school's framework to a German-language audience, playing a similar pedagogical role to that of Hanegraaff's Western Esotericism: A Guide for the Perplexed (2012) in English.
