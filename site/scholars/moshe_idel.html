Moshe Idel

Scholarly Authority

Moshe Idel (born 1947 in Târgu Neamț, Romania), Israeli scholar of Jewish mysticism at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, is the most important revisionist of Gershom Scholem's foundational history of Kabbalah and one of the central figures in scholarship on the intersection of Jewish mysticism with Renaissance Christian Hermetism. His first major work, Kabbalah: New Perspectives (1988), challenged Scholem's synthesis at its methodological foundations, arguing that Scholem's history had overemphasised the literary and Gnostic dimensions of the tradition at the expense of its ecstatic, theurgical, and performative dimensions. The figure of Abraham Abulafia (1240–c.1291), the Spanish Kabbalist who developed an elaborate system of letter-permutation and breath-control meditation aimed at prophetic experience, became Idel's paradigm case for a strand of Kabbalah that Scholem had marginalised. Idel's scholarship has subsequently reshaped the study of Jewish mysticism, Hermetism, and the Renaissance encounter between Jewish and Christian intellectual cultures.

Central Argument

Idel's revisionism proceeds on two fronts. Against Scholem's emphasis on Kabbalah's Gnostic content and its textual transmission, Idel insists on the primacy of practice: Kabbalah is not primarily a body of esoteric doctrine but a set of techniques — linguistic, contemplative, and theurgical — aimed at transforming the practitioner's relation to the divine. Where Scholem saw a single grand narrative moving from Gnostic origins through the Zohar to the Safed school of Luria, Idel sees multiple, relatively autonomous traditions pursuing different experiential and theological goals. His second major argument concerns the interaction between Kabbalah and Renaissance Christian thought. Where earlier scholarship had treated Christian Kabbalah as a superficial adoption of Jewish materials for Christian theological purposes, Idel argues for genuine intellectual exchange in which Kabbalistic ideas about language, sefirot, and divine names shaped the philosophical frameworks of Renaissance Christian thinkers in substantive ways. His work on Giovanni Pico della Mirandola in Kabbalah in Italy (2011) demonstrates that Pico's engagement with Kabbalistic sources was more technically informed than previously acknowledged. This research has major implications for understanding the synthesis of Hermetism and Kabbalah in the Florentine Neoplatonic circle where Ficino and Pico collaborated, since it establishes that the Kabbalistic strand of that synthesis was not merely ornamental but carried genuine philosophical weight.

Key Works

Kabbalah: New Perspectives (New Haven: Yale University Press, 1988) is the programmatic statement of Idel's revisionist methodology, introducing the categories of "theosophic-theurgical" and "ecstatic" Kabbalah as two major currents Scholem had illegitimately conflated. The Mystical Experience in Abraham Abulafia (Albany: SUNY Press, 1988) provides the detailed case study through Abulafia's techniques of letter-combination and linguistic meditation. Golem: Jewish Magical and Mystical Traditions on the Artificial Anthropoid (Albany: SUNY Press, 1990) traces the golem motif through Kabbalistic, magical, and philosophical literature. Absorbing Perfections: Kabbalah and Interpretation (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2002) addresses the hermeneutical theory underpinning Kabbalistic reading practices. Kabbalah in Italy, 1280–1510 (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2011) provides the most detailed account of the Italian-Jewish context for Pico's Kabbalah.

Scholarly Lineage and Disputes

Idel's most sustained dispute is with Scholem's legacy as maintained by scholars such as Yehuda Liebes and Elliot Wolfson, who defend the primacy of the theosophical-mythological dimension of the Zohar against Idel's ecstatic alternative. His engagement with Christian Kabbalah places him in dialogue with Brian Copenhaver on Pico and with Wouter Hanegraaff on the broader history of Western esotericism. His methodological debts are to Scholem himself — he is a genuine heir who revises from within — but also to historians of religion such as Mircea Eliade and to the phenomenology of religious experience.
