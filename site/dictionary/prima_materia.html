Prima Materia

ALCHEMICAL Concept · ACTOR TERM
Encyclopedia entry for Prima Materia← Browse relational connections

Prima materia — Latin for "first matter" — is an Actor Term of central importance in the alchemical tradition, designating the primordial, undifferentiated substrate from which all metals and composite substances are held to be made and to which they can be reduced by the alchemical art. As an Actor Term, it was explicitly employed by alchemists from the Arabic period through the Renaissance and beyond, and it connects two distinct but related registers: a philosophical claim about the ultimate constitution of matter, derived from Aristotle's doctrine of prime matter (hyle), and a practical claim about the starting material of the alchemical opus — the raw substance from which the philosopher's stone is to be extracted or upon which it is to work. The deliberate obscurity with which alchemical authors described the prima materia — identifying it variously as a common, despised substance hidden in plain sight, as a universal spirit present in all things, or as a specific material such as mercury or sulfur — is not merely mystification but reflects a genuine theoretical difficulty: if the first matter is truly undifferentiated, it cannot be simply identical with any one differentiated material substance.

Historical Usage

The philosophical background of the prima materia concept lies in Aristotle's doctrine of prime matter (prote hyle), developed principally in the Physics and the Metaphysics. For Aristotle, prime matter is the ultimate substratum of all natural change: when one substance is transformed into another — water into air, wood into fire — something persists through the change as the subject of transformation. This persistent substratum, stripped of all form and quality, is prime matter. It is never found in pure, unqualified form in nature — it is always already formed and qualified — but it must be posited as the logical correlate of substantial change. The Aristotelian prime matter is thus a philosophical abstraction, not a concrete material, and this abstract character was inherited and transformed by the alchemical tradition.

Jabir ibn Hayyan (c. 721–c. 815), the foundational theorist of Arabic alchemy, developed the sulfur-mercury theory of metals that became the dominant alchemical account of metallic composition throughout the medieval and Renaissance periods. On this theory, all metals are composed of two principles — a sulphureous principle that determines the metal's combustibility and colour, and a mercurial principle that determines its fusibility and lustre — combined in different proportions and degrees of purity. The prima materia in this framework is the common substrate from which both sulfur and mercury (in their philosophical, not vulgar, forms) are derived, and which underlies all metallic differentiation. The alchemist's task, on the Jabiran model, is to reduce the impure metals back to this common substrate and then re-form it under more favorable conditions, producing the perfect proportion and purity of sulfur and mercury that constitutes gold.

In the Latin alchemical tradition, the prima materia acquired a multiplicity of names that the texts both multiply and obscure. The thirteenth-century Rosarium Philosophorum — a compilation text that became one of the most widely circulated alchemical works in the medieval and Renaissance West — refers to it as chaos, as the massa confusa (confused mass), as lead, as quicksilver, as the dragon, and as many other designations, adding the characteristic instruction that the true prima materia is found everywhere, costs nothing, and is despised by the ignorant. This rhetoric of the hidden and ubiquitous — the prima materia as a common substance that the untrained eye overlooks — served both to protect the art from the uninitiated and to express a genuine philosophical point: the ultimate substrate of material existence is not itself any particular material thing.

Paracelsus (Theophrastus Bombastus von Hohenheim, 1493/94–1541) effected a decisive transformation of the prima materia concept by replacing the Aristotelian-Jabiran sulfur-mercury theory with his own doctrine of three principles: sulfur, mercury, and salt, the tria prima. For Paracelsus, these are not the vulgar materials but philosophical principles — combustibility, volatility, and solidity or fixity — that together constitute all material bodies. The underlying prima materia in the Paracelsian system is sometimes identified with the biblical iliastrum or Iliaster, the primordial substance from which God's creative Word differentiated all things at the beginning of creation. This Paracelsian move gives the prima materia a theological dimension — it becomes a creaturely reflection of the creative potency of the divine Word — that was elaborated by later Paracelsians and that fed into the broader tradition of Christian theosophy.

Scholarly Significance

Lawrence M. Principe's The Secrets of Alchemy (University of Chicago Press, 2013) and his earlier collaborative work with William R. Newman, Alchemy Tried in the Fire (2002), have substantially transformed the scholarly understanding of what alchemists meant by the prima materia by insisting on reading alchemical texts as seriously intentional accounts of chemical operations rather than as symbolic or spiritual allegories. Principe's "chymical" approach — treating alchemy as an early form of practical chemistry rather than a primarily spiritual or mystical discipline — has recovered the concrete material referents that alchemical terminology often obscures. On Principe's reading, the sulfur-mercury theory and the doctrine of prima materia are not primarily philosophical abstractions but operational hypotheses guiding laboratory practice, and understanding what alchemists actually did in their laboratories is essential for understanding what they meant by their theoretical terms.

William R. Newman's work on Roger Bacon, Albert the Great, and the Latin alchemical tradition — particularly Promethean Ambitions: Alchemy and the Quest to Perfect Nature (2004) — has traced the sophisticated philosophical engagement with the prima materia concept in thirteenth and fourteenth-century natural philosophy, showing that the leading scholastic discussions of matter, form, and transmutation were deeply engaged with alchemical questions and that the boundary between legitimate natural philosophy and suspect alchemy was far more permeable than later historians assumed. Barbara Obrist's work on the visual imagery of medieval alchemical manuscripts has analyzed how the prima materia was depicted in illuminated alchemical texts, showing that the imagery of chaos, the dragon, the black earth, and the shapeless prima substance that pervades this iconographic tradition expresses a coherent cosmological vision rather than mere decorative elaboration.

Wouter J. Hanegraaff's account of the alchemical tradition in Esotericism and the Academy (Cambridge, 2012) situates the prima materia within the broader history of "rejected knowledge" in Western culture, arguing that alchemy's claim to transmute base metals into gold was not primarily a fraud or a delusion but an expression of a coherent natural philosophy whose premises — the possibility of substantial transformation, the existence of a common substratum of matter, the participation of material processes in a divine creative order — were taken seriously by many of the most learned figures of the medieval and Renaissance periods.

Transmission and Variant Forms

The Arabic prima materia concept — rendered as al-hayula al-ula, directly translating the Greek prote hyle — entered Latin alchemical literature through the twelfth-century translation movement centered in Toledo, alongside works attributed to Jabir and the texts of the Aristotelian natural philosophy tradition. The Latin reception created a characteristic conflation of the philosophical prima materia with concrete alchemical starting materials — mercury, lead, antimony, common sulfur, vitriol — that varied enormously from author to author and that gave the concept its characteristic obscurity and multiplicity. The effort to identify the "true" prima materia was a recurring preoccupation of alchemical literature precisely because the texts gave conflicting and deliberately encoded answers.

Related Concepts

The prima materia concept is inseparable from the broader alchemical theory of the philosopher's stone, which acts upon the prima materia as its perfecting agent. The doctrine of solve et coagula — dissolution and coagulation — describes the fundamental operations performed on the prima materia in the course of the alchemical work. The Paracelsian modification of the concept, replacing it with the tria prima of sulfur, mercury, and salt, represents the most significant early modern transformation of the prima materia doctrine.
